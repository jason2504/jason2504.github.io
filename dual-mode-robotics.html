Dual-Mode Robotic Systems

For Manipulation and Locomotion — GRASP Lab, University of Pennsylvania

Key Achievement

Designed and prototyped a novel dual-mode robotic system capable of both precision manipulation (250g payload) and dynamic locomotion support, through three parallel arm architectures: a rigid joint system with integrated universal joint shoulder (V1), a biomimetic cable-actuated continuum arm (V2), and a Franka Panda-inspired serial manipulator (V3). All versions share a validated compliant 3-finger worm-gear gripper that adapts to objects ranging from a marble to an apple. A wearable "Dr. Oct" backpack variant was also designed for assistive robotics applications.

🤖 Research ongoing at the University of Pennsylvania GRASP Laboratory under Dr. Nadia Figueroa, targeting search & rescue, hazardous inspection, and assistive robotics.

Project Overview

This research addresses a fundamental gap: the lack of a cost-effective system excelling at both precision manipulation and dynamic locomotion. Quadrupeds like Boston Dynamics' Spot excel at locomotion; industrial arms excel at manipulation; humanoids excel at both but are prohibitively expensive. Drawing inspiration from Dr. Octopus from Marvel comics and from primates that use their limbs for both locomotion and manipulation, this project explores dual-function limb designs through three parallel architectures — each contributing unique insight into the manipulation–locomotion trade-off space.

Rather than iterating linearly, all three versions were developed concurrently to explore different points in the design space. The team consisted of a PhD student and Master's student focused on controls, while I led all mechanical and electrical design.

Design Criteria & Constraints

Measurable success criteria were established from day one: each arm must grasp at least 250g, workspaces must overlap for multi-arm collaboration and DOF redundancy, the system must stay under 10kg with each arm under 1kg, and FDM 3D printing with PLA/TPU is used for rapid iteration. Smart servo motors with built-in microcontrollers provide closed-loop control. Safety factors of 2–5× are applied for dynamic loads. Material properties are adjusted from bulk PLA values (E = 1830 MPa) to reflect 15% infill with 4 exterior walls (E = 1464 MPa, ρ = 0.66 g/cm³) for accurate SolidWorks mass and FEA analysis.

Version 1: Universal Joint Rigid Manipulator

Key Innovation: Integrated 2-DOF Shoulder

Traditional designs use two separate motors mounted perpendicular for a 2-DOF shoulder. V1 combines both degrees of freedom into a single mechanism using a timing belt and bevel gear coupling for 90-degree power transmission — reducing motor count while maintaining full shoulder mobility. The 4-DOF configuration (2 at shoulder, 1 at elbow, 1 at wrist) was intentional: simpler control, fewer failure points, lower cost, and reduced weight, with multi-arm coordination compensating for the reduced individual workspace.

Torque Analysis

Worst-case loading was modeled with the arm fully extended horizontally at 250g payload. The arm was split into four sections — universal joint (78.07g), elbow joint (74.14g), wrist joint (106.96g), and gripper with dummy load (397.75g) — with torque calculated backwards from the end-effector to each motor using SolidWorks mass properties for precise center-of-mass distances. Maximum required torque: 1.55 N·m at the shoulder, verified with a 3× safety factor. AGMA bevel gear bending and contact stress analysis confirmed PLA gears would fail at rated load, while steel gears (safety factor 2.8×) are adequate for production.

Lessons Learned

Three key issues informed subsequent versions: (1) compact link lengths limited workspace — accepted weight trade-off, informed V3 scaling; (2) timing belt tensioning was difficult to maintain — spring-loaded idler pulleys identified as the solution; (3) backlash in 3D-printed bevel gears was acceptable for prototyping but production needs split gears or machined alternatives. Most critically, the coupled universal joint motors were difficult to control simultaneously: more independent joints is easier to control than fewer coupled joints.

Version 2: Cable-Actuated Continuum Robot

Key Innovation: Biomimetic Continuum Spine

Version 2 takes a fundamentally different approach — continuous bending instead of discrete rigid joints, with inherent compliance. The spine consists of 6 triangular vertebrae spaced 40mm apart, connected by ball-and-socket joints (ball radius 5mm, socket radius 5.4mm, socket opening 4.6mm → maximum geometric deflection 67°). Custom TPU compression springs (k = 0.005 N/mm, 19mm × 25.8mm, printed to geometry) between vertebrae provide structural support and compliance analogous to intervertebral discs. Actual operation targets 30–40° deflection per joint, enabling a full 180° spine bend. The two-arm configuration spans 950mm.

Actuation uses 3 Dynamixel motors driving 0.3mm fishing-line tendons routed through vertebra corners. Torsional strip springs at cable terminations maintain constant pre-tension to compensate for fishing line stretch under load.

Challenges & Solutions

V2 is inherently underactuated — 3 motors drive a theoretically infinite-DOF system — making accurate simulation impossible and requiring real-world reinforcement learning. Cable slack is addressed with torsional strip springs; guide rails prevent torsional spring entanglement during continuous rotation. Symmetric 120° cable spacing with TPU compression springs solves spine stability under self-weight. While V2 demonstrated 180° bend physically, the RL framework proved difficult to develop, motivating the pivot to V3 as a more tractable control platform.

Version 3: Franka Panda-Inspired Serial Manipulator

Design Rationale

After V1 revealed coupled-joint control difficulty and V2 proved hard to simulate, the team needed proven kinematics with no mechanical unknowns. The solution: adapt the open-source Franka Panda robot arm — an industry-standard manipulator used by the FACTR research group — with shortened link lengths for weight reduction and the final wrist DOF removed as redundant.

The 5-DOF configuration uses tiered motor selection driven by joint torque requirements: larger Dynamixel XM430 motors at joints 1 and 3 (longest gravitational moment arms) and smaller XC330 motors at joints 4 and 5. Joints 0 and 2, despite being at the base, do not experience the full gravitational moment because their rotation axes are not perpendicular to gravity. Despite having 5 DOF vs. V1's 4, V3 weighs only 716g vs. V1's 828g — eliminating the heavy universal joint mechanism more than offsets the additional joints. This confirmed that compactness does not always mean lighter weight.

Universal Compliant Gripper

A single gripper design spans all three arm versions, satisfying both locomotion (0° fully open for ground contact) and manipulation (120° closed for enveloping grasp) requirements. At ~150g, it is the lightest element on the arm. One motor at the center drives all 3 fingers simultaneously through a two-stage worm gear reduction (30:1 worm-to-large-wheel × 3:1 large-to-small = 90:1 total), providing high torque amplification and self-locking capability.

An extension spring behind each large phalange allows independent finger adaptation to object geometry without individual motor control: when the lower phalange contacts an object, the upper phalange continues rotating to wrap around it. Each finger is independently removable; springs hook rather than using adhesive. Validation covered objects from a marble to an apple — spherical, cylindrical, block, and deformable shapes — with grip security confirmed by yanking the held object.

Electronics Architecture

All three arm versions share the same electronics housed in a 170mm × 170mm × 160mm central torso with a hinge-lid enclosure for easy access. Core components: NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX for central computation (10–25W, configurable); Adafruit BNO085 IMU for real-time orientation estimation, gravity compensation for arm kinematics, and tilt detection; dual USB fisheye cameras for stereo depth perception, object detection, and environment mapping; four U2D2 Power Hub modules connected via USB to the Jetson's USB-A ports managing serial communication to all servos.

A dual-battery architecture isolates servo power (3S LiPo, 14.8V, two 5200 mAh motor packs at the lumbar position for optimal center of mass) from logic power (21700 cells, 2200 mAh, direct to Jetson via barrel jack adapter), preventing motor switching noise from corrupting sensor data. Caster wheels at the base of each arm enable rolling locomotion when the gripper is open.

Wearable Variant: "Dr. Oct" Backpack Robot

Beyond the floor-based platform, I designed a wearable backpack variant re-packaging the same V3 arms and electronics into a body-worn configuration. The motivation: assist a human operator in tight search-and-rescue scenarios (pushing aside debris, holding tools, reaching overhead) while the user's own hands perform other tasks. The design also has a daily-life assistive angle — supporting objects while doing other tasks, or providing reach assistance.

The torso takes a pyramidal form inspired by the original Dr. Octopus arms from Spider-Man, with four arms mounting symmetrically outward: two upper arms wrap up and over the shoulders, two lower arms wrap around the waist. The pyramidal torso houses all electronics and batteries internally. The system is worn via nylon webbing shoulder straps sized to average male shoulder width (40 cm). Power uses a 4S LiPo for servo current and a separate 2S LiPo for the logic domain.

Manufacturing, Scalability & Future Work

Manufacturing Scalability

FDM 3D printing (PLA/TPU) is ideal for the current research phase — no tooling, 3–5 day iteration cycles. Some features are uniquely enabled by 3D printing (V2's ball-and-socket joints, TPU snap-fit compression springs) and would require redesign for other methods. For low-volume production (1–100 units), CNC machining provides isotropic strength and tighter gear tolerances. For scale (500+ units), injection molding achieves 30–60s cycles with $15K–50K tooling and required design changes (draft angles, undercut elimination, post-machined threaded holes).
